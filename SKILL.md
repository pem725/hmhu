  Help Me Help You (HMHU). Takes a comment, post, email, or argument someone
  else wrote — brilliant, asinine, or somewhere in between — and breaks it into
  its claims, its assumptions, and the emotion underneath it. Then drafts a
  short reply that thanks the person for something specific, offers where you
  have struggled and where you remain uncertain, adds one thing of real value,
  and asks one question you actually want answered. Never tells the other
  person they are being emotional. Includes a don't-send gate for the times
  when silence is the wiser move. Built for the AI debate and for comment
  sections generally, but works on any thread where people are talking past
  each other. Use when someone says "help me respond to this", "how should I
  reply", "someone said this on my post", "break down this argument", "is this
  worth replying to", "what's the assumption here", "HMHU", or pastes a comment
  and asks what to do with it.

# Help Me Help You

## The stance

The name is the whole method. *I want to take you seriously — give me something
to work with.*

Not: here is why you are wrong. Not: here is a polite way to say you are
irrational. The reply this skill produces is an invitation to keep talking,
written by someone who might be the one who is wrong.

And it comes from a good heart, not a judgmental heart. That distinction is not
decoration. It is the difference between the version of this that works and the
version that makes everything worse, and it shows up in the output within two
sentences.

## The engine is a question

An argument gives an emotional state a target. A question gives it a task.

That is the whole mechanism. Answering a question — even silently, even in
order to refuse it — switches on the machinery that the emotional state had
switched off. People argue their way *deeper* into a position and think their
way *out* of one. Which means the question outperforms the rebuttal even when
the rebuttal is correct, and especially then.

So the question is not the polite closing move of the reply. It is the reply.
Everything else is context for it.

**Three conditions, or it doesn't work:**

1. **It has to be real.** A question you already know the answer to is a trap,
   and people feel the trap before they parse the question. Curiosity performed
   as technique is interrogation.
2. **It should be about them, not the topic.** *"What made you feel you had to
   write that?"* outperforms *"what's your evidence?"* The second is a summons.
   The first is an interest.
3. **It has to be answerable in a sentence.** Big questions read as homework
   and get no reply, which teaches you nothing.

**The structural rule that follows: no real question, no reply.** If you cannot
find a question you would be glad to get an answer to, you have stopped being
curious and started scoring. That is not a writing problem, it is the
retribution reading in disguise — send nothing. This check is not optional and
it is the most useful thing in this skill.

**The good-heart test:** would you be *pleased* to be wrong about them? If the
honest answer is no, the question is a weapon and it will land as one, however
it is phrased.

Curiosity also costs something. It requires tolerating not knowing — for the
length of the exchange, in public, in front of people keeping score. That
tolerance is the part most people don't have, not the questions. See
`references/curiosity.md`.

## The one rule that keeps this honest

**Run the emotional audit on yourself first, and never ship the diagnosis.**

A tool that names other people's emotions is a contempt machine with good
manners. "You seem to be reacting from fear" is unfalsifiable and unanswerable;
it is a worse move than a plain argument, because it denies the other person a
way to respond that isn't proving your point.

So the analysis has two audiences and they never mix:

| Output | Reader | Contains |
|---|---|---|
| **The breakdown** | You, only | Claims, assumptions, emotional read — theirs *and* yours |
| **The reply** | Them | Specific thanks, something given, one honest question |

The emotional read informs the reply's *shape*. It never appears in its text.

## The three minds, stated correctly

DBT's frame: **emotion mind** decides by how it feels, **reasonable mind**
decides by the facts, **wise mind** is the overlap — not the absence of
emotion, the integration of it. Wise mind is not the person with no feelings.
It is the person whose feelings are in the room and not at the wheel.

This matters because the naive version of this skill — *emotion bad, logic
good* — produces smug, bloodless replies that convince no one, and it is
factually wrong about people. Some of the best things anyone does are driven
entirely by emotion. The question is never *is there emotion here*. There
always is. The question is **what the emotion is doing**.

## What the emotion is doing: four readings

Sort the feeling underneath a comment into one of these. Sort your own the same
way, first.

**1. Signal.** The emotion is pointing at something real — a value being
stepped on, a harm being waved away, a loss the person can genuinely see
coming. This is emotion doing its job. It is information you do not have.
→ *In the reply: name the concern. Never the emotion. "There's a real risk that
the people who lose work here are the ones with the least room to absorb it" —
not "I can tell you're worried."*

**2. Armor.** The emotion is protecting the person from a threat — to their
livelihood, their competence, their status, their sense of what they are for.
Legitimate in origin, distorting in effect: it makes them argue against the
weakest version of the thing, because the strong version is unbearable.
→ *In the reply: lower the threat before you raise the argument. Concede the
part that is true. Attacking armor thickens it.*

**3. Retribution.** The emotion has found someone to make pay. Contempt,
dunking, scorekeeping, the pleasure of the well-aimed line. This is the one
that harms third parties, and it is the most common reason to send nothing.
→ *In the reply: usually, there is no reply. See the don't-send gate.*

**4. Momentum.** The emotion is borrowed from the thread, not from the person.
They are matching the register of the six comments above them. This is the
cheapest to defuse and the most common in comment sections: change the register
and people change with you. It is why one thoughtful comment can turn a pile-on
— which is the entire reason this skill exists.

Positive feeling is on this list too. *I loved this comment and want to say so*
is momentum. It is a fine reason to reply. It is not a reason to skip the audit.

## Procedure

1. **Read it twice.** Once for what it says, once for what it is defending.
2. **Audit yourself.** What do you feel, and which of the four is it? Write it
   down. If it is retribution, stop here and go to the gate.
3. **Split their comment** into: explicit claims, the assumptions holding them
   up, and the one thing in it that is *true* — there is almost always one, and
   in a good comment there are several.
4. **Locate the wise mind in what they wrote.** Where were they already
   reasoning well? This is what you thank them for, and it must be specific
   enough that the sentence could not be pasted under a different comment.
5. **Find the one thing you can give.** A fact they would want. A distinction
   that helps their own argument. A place you were wrong. A place you still
   are on the fence. Give it away without charging for it.
6. **Write the question first.** One sentence, real, about them, answerable
   briefly. If it will not come, that is the answer — go to the gate.
7. **Draft around it, then cut.** See the craft rules.
8. **Run the gate.** Then hand over the breakdown and the reply, separately.

## Reply craft

**Length.** Comment section: 120 words, hard. Email or long-form: 200. If it
does not fit, the reply is doing two jobs and one of them is showing off.

**Shape.** Three moves, in this order:
- The specific true thing they said, named back to them precisely.
- Something given — data, a concession, a distinction, an uncertainty of yours.
- **One question you would actually be glad to get an answer to.** This is the
  load-bearing sentence. Write it first, then write the rest around it.

**Never:**
- Name their emotion, or gesture at it ("I understand this is frustrating").
- "That's a valid point" without saying which point and why.
- Open with "Actually," or "To be fair," or "With respect,".
- Cite a study you cannot name.
- End on a rhetorical question. If you would be annoyed by any honest answer,
  it is not a question, it is a closing argument.
- Claim more certainty than you have. If you are on the fence, say which post
  you are leaning against.
- Sound like a press release. If the draft has three-item lists, "it's not just
  X, it's Y", or an em dash in every sentence, run the `humanizer` skill on it.

**The generosity test.** Does this reply make the other person look better to
the room, or worse? If worse, rewrite it. A reply that wins and humiliates has
lost — everyone reading learns that engaging you is expensive.

## The don't-send gate

Silence is a legitimate output of this skill. Do not send if:

- **Your read of yourself came back retribution.** Wait a day. The reply will
  either improve or become unnecessary.
- **They are performing, not arguing.** You cannot reach someone mid-
  performance; you can only become part of the act. Tell: the comment is
  addressed to the audience, not to anyone in particular.
- **You are defending a third party who did not ask.** Sometimes right. Often
  it is retribution wearing a cause.
- **You would not write it if no one could see it.**
- **It is the third exchange.** Two is a conversation. Three is a position.
- **You could not find a real question.** See above. This one is not a judgment
  call — if the question isn't there, the curiosity isn't either.

When the gate closes, say so plainly and say which criterion, then offer the
breakdown anyway — understanding the comment is worth something even when
answering it is not.

## Whose uncertainties go in the reply

The "here is where I have struggled" move only works with real material.

Read `references/my-position.md` for the user's standing positions and open
questions. If that file is missing or still has its placeholders, do not invent
positions for them — ask, in one question, what they actually think and where
they are genuinely unsure, then write it into that file for next time.
