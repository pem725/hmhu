# Technology adoption: the numbers, and the honest caveats

## Read this first

**Every figure below is from memory and approximate.** Verify before citing any
of it in public — a wrong number in an AI argument becomes the whole
conversation, and deservedly. Use these as pointers to what to look up, not as
citations.

Sources worth going to: US Census / Historical Statistics of the United States
for household penetration, Pew Research for internet and smartphone adoption,
Our World in Data's technology-diffusion series (which compiles most of this).

## US household penetration, years to ~50%

| Technology | Introduced | ~50% of households | Approx. years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telephone | 1876 | mid-1940s | ~70 |
| Electricity | 1882 | mid-1920s | ~40 |
| Automobile | ~1900 | late 1920s | ~30 |
| Radio | 1920 | early 1930s | ~10 |
| Television | ~1946 | mid-1950s | ~10 |
| Personal computer | ~1977 | late 1990s | ~20 |
| Internet (adults) | ~1995 | ~2000 | ~5–8 |
| Cell phone (adults) | 1983 | mid-2000s | ~20 |
| Smartphone (adults) | 2007 | ~2012 | ~5 |

The pattern people usually want from this table: **later technologies diffuse
faster**, because the complementary infrastructure already exists. The
telephone needed wires strung to every house. The smartphone needed an app
store.

## The best single fact in this file

**The dynamo.** Factories electrified decades before electricity showed up in
productivity statistics. The reason is not inertia — it is that the gains
required redesigning the factory. Steam plants drove machines through a central
shaft and belts, so machines had to be arranged around the power source.
Electric motors let each machine have its own, which meant the floor could be
laid out around the *work* instead. That reorganization took a generation and
the retirement of the buildings themselves.

Paul David's "The Dynamo and the Computer" (1990) is the paper. Robert Solow's
1987 line — *"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity
statistics"* — is the same observation about computers, and Erik Brynjolfsson's
"productivity J-curve" work is the modern formalization: general-purpose
technologies show *negative* measured productivity first, because the
complementary investment is real and the output of it is not yet counted.

This is the fact to hand a thoughtful commenter arguing adoption lag. It
supports their point, it is more specific than what they said, and it is
genuinely interesting.

## The counter-evidence — do not skip this

The adoption-lag argument is not a trump card, and using it as one is exactly
the failure this skill exists to avoid. Honest version:

**Survivorship.** The table lists technologies that won. Adoption curves for
the picturephone, the Segway, Google Glass, 3D television, and Betamax are
flat lines. "It's early" is what people said about those too, and it was not
evidence of anything.

**Slow adoption is sometimes correct.** Sometimes people are not
under-informed; they have priced in a cost the enthusiasts have not. Leaded
gasoline, asbestos, and the early opioid formulations were adopted *quickly*.
Speed is not a quality signal in either direction.

**"Same fears as last time" is a bad argument.** It is technically a claim of
historical similarity and it functions as a claim of *guaranteed outcome*, and
it tells the other person their concern is unoriginal. It entrenches every
person it is aimed at. It also happens to be circular: previous fears being
unfounded does not make this one unfounded, and some previous fears were
correct — the ones about coal, cars and cities, and social media each had a
naysayer who was right and ignored.

**The distributional point stands regardless.** Even where a technology is a
net good over fifty years, the costs and benefits land on different people, and
the people they land on are not the same people. "It worked out" is a statement
about an aggregate. Almost nobody lives in an aggregate.

## Where the current AI numbers sit

Roughly a third of US adults reported having used ChatGPT in Pew's 2024–25
surveys, with usage concentrated in younger and higher-education groups.
Workplace adoption figures vary wildly by survey depending on whether they ask
about *any* use or *regular* use — a gap large enough that citing the high
number without the qualifier is a form of lying. **Check the current figures
before using any of this; this file's knowledge has a cutoff.**
