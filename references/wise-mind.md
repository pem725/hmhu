# Wise mind, without the smugness

## The frame

DBT's three states:

- **Emotion mind** — the decision is made by how it feels. Fast, sometimes
  right, poor at revising.
- **Reasonable mind** — the decision is made by the facts. Accurate about
  quantities, blind to what matters.
- **Wise mind** — the overlap. Not the absence of feeling. The integration of
  it.

Reasonable mind alone is not the goal state. Reasonable mind alone is how you
get technically correct decisions that everyone involved hates, and it is
overrepresented in exactly the arguments this skill is used on.

## The two ways this frame goes wrong

**1. Using it as a scoreboard.** "I'm in wise mind, you're in emotion mind" is
the most annoying sentence in the English language and it is never true when
said out loud. Anyone certain they are in wise mind is in emotion mind about
being in wise mind. The frame is a lens for looking at yourself. Pointed
outward and spoken aloud, it is a slur with a citation.

**2. Treating emotion as error.** Emotion is frequently the most accurate thing
in the room. Someone whose read is *"this is going to be used to cut costs and
call it progress"* is not failing at reasoning — they are reporting a pattern
they have watched play out, and the feeling is the summary of the data. The
failure mode is not *feeling* it. It is letting it decide what is true about
the specific case in front of them.

## Signal, armor, retribution, momentum

The four readings from SKILL.md, with more room:

### Signal
Emotion is tracking something real. The person is often ahead of you: the
feeling arrived before the argument because the pattern was recognized before
it was articulable.

*Test:* can you state the underlying concern as a factual claim that might be
true? If yes, it is signal. *"AI tools will be adopted fastest by the
organizations least accountable for the harm"* is a claim. It might be right.

*Reply move:* state the concern back better than they stated it. This is the
single highest-value thing you can do in a thread, and almost nobody does it.

### Armor
Emotion is defending against a threat. The tell: they are arguing against a
weak version of the thing that nobody proposed. They are not being stupid; the
strong version is unbearable, so they engage the version they can beat.

*Reply move:* lower the threat first. Concede the real part explicitly and
early. Do not "steelman" at them — that is armor-piercing ammunition with
manners. Give them somewhere safe to stand and they will often walk to it
themselves.

### Retribution
Emotion has found someone to make pay. Contempt, dunking, scorekeeping.

*Tell in others:* the comment is written for the audience, not the addressee.
*Tell in yourself:* you have a phrase you are pleased with and you are building
the reply around it. That phrase is the reply's actual purpose. Delete it and
see whether anything remains.

*Reply move:* usually none. This is the case where replying feeds it. The
exception is when a third party is being harmed and someone should visibly not
be enjoying it — but check whether that is your reason or your cover.

### Momentum
Emotion borrowed from the thread. People match the register above them.

*Reply move:* this is the good news. Register is contagious in both directions,
and a single comment written in a different key can turn a pile-on — one
person's thoughtfulness gives everyone else permission to stop performing.
Most of the wins available here are this.

## The generosity test

The last check before sending, and the one that catches what the others miss:

**Does this reply make the other person look better to the room, or worse?**

If worse, rewrite it — even if it is true, even if they started it, even if
they deserve it. A reply that wins and humiliates teaches every reader that
engaging you is expensive. You will win that thread and have a smaller
conversation for the rest of your life.
