## The mechanism

Curiosity is **approach** motivation. Defensiveness is **avoidance**
motivation. They pull in opposite directions, and holding both at once takes
effort most people will not spend in a comment thread. So you do not have to
argue someone out of a defensive state. You have to give them something to
approach.

A question does that and an argument cannot:

- An argument hands the emotional state a **target**. It confirms that a fight
  was the right read of the situation, and it recruits everything the person
  has already built for fights.
- A question hands it a **task**. Answering — even silently, even in order to
  refuse — requires retrieving, comparing, and reporting. That is reflective
  machinery, and it is the machinery the emotional state had turned down.

This is also why people argue their way *deeper* into positions. Defending a
claim rehearses it; each defense makes the next one cheaper and the retreat
more expensive. Nobody has ever been talked out of a position they just spent
four paragraphs defending. Get there before the paragraphs.

## Two kinds of curiosity, and which one you are talking to

Kashdan's five-dimensional model separates dimensions that get flattened in
ordinary use. Two matter here:

**Deprivation sensitivity** — curiosity as an itch. There is a gap, the gap is
aversive, and closing it is a relief. This is the mode most people are in when
they pile onto a thread: they are not exploring, they are *resolving*. Being
right is the relief.

**Joyous exploration** — curiosity as appetite. The gap is interesting rather
than intolerable. Nothing needs to close.

A good question can move someone from the first to the second, because it
reframes the unknown as *interesting* instead of *unfinished*. A bad question
deepens the first: it makes the gap feel larger and more urgent, which reads as
threat, which is where you started.

**Stress tolerance** is the dimension that gates all of it: the willingness to
sit in the anxiety of not knowing. This is why saying *"I genuinely don't know
where I land on this"* in your own reply does real work. You are not being
humble for points. You are demonstrating that the unresolved state is
survivable, in public, at no apparent cost. Some people have never watched
anyone do that.

*(Verify the dimension names and check whether the deprivation → exploration
shift is empirically supported or a plausible extrapolation before this claim
goes anywhere public.)*

## Make the gap small and visible

Loewenstein's information-gap account: curiosity comes from awareness of a
*specific* gap, not from general ignorance. Small, well-defined gaps produce
curiosity. Enormous ones produce anxiety and avoidance.

Practical consequence for the reply:

- **"What would change your mind?"** is a huge gap. It reads as homework or as
  a trap.
- **"Was there a specific tool that burned you, or is it the whole direction?"**
  is a small one. It is answerable in six words and it is genuinely useful to
  know.

Ask the smallest question whose answer you would actually want.

## Question taxonomy

**Origin.** *"Where did you first run into this?"* / *"What made you feel you
had to write that?"* — nearly always the best opening. It asks about their
experience, which they are the authority on, so there is no wrong answer and
therefore no defense required.

**Condition.** *"What would have to be true for this to go well?"* — moves from
verdict to model. Hard to answer angrily.

**Symmetric.** *"What would change your mind, and what should change mine?"* —
the second clause is what makes it not a trap. Never ask the first half alone.

**Cost.** *"What's the part of this that would actually cost you something?"* —
gets past the general objection to the specific stake. This is where the real
disagreement usually lives, and often it is smaller than the thread suggests.

## What not to ask

- **The Socratic trap.** A question you know the answer to, laid so they walk
  into it. Everyone can feel this. It converts a disagreement into a
  humiliation, and it teaches the audience that your questions are traps.
- **Sealioning.** Endless polite questions as a denial-of-service. The tell is
  that no answer would ever be enough. If you would ask a follow-up regardless
  of what they said, you are not curious.
- **The disguised assertion.** *"Don't you think it's a bit convenient that…"*
  That is a claim with a question mark. Say the claim or ask the question.
- **The stacked ask.** Three questions in one comment is an interrogation. One.

## Turning it inward

The same move works on yourself, and it is cheaper to practice there.

When you notice the pull to reply, ask before you draft:

1. What am I feeling, and what is it doing — signal, armor, retribution,
   momentum?
2. What would I have to believe for their comment to be reasonable? Not *is it*
   reasonable. What would have to be true.
3. What is the smallest thing I am actually unsure about here?

Question 3 is the one that changes the reply, because whatever it turns up is
the thing worth giving away in the second move. Your live uncertainty is the
most persuasive thing you have and the only one nobody can accuse you of
performing.
